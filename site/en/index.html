Alikhani–Griswold, Section 4(3): connected graphs with domination root −4

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

The manuscript asks whether connected networks can grow arbitrarily large while a polynomial counting their covering selections still vanishes at minus four. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang answer yes by linking copies of an earlier example without changing which selections provide coverage. They also construct examples of every size from thirty-five vertices onward, while keeping the number of neighbors of any vertex at most seven.

Here a graph is a finite network with undirected edges, no loops and no repeated edges. A selection of vertices dominates the graph if every vertex is selected or neighbors a selected vertex; the selection itself need not be connected. The domination polynomial D(G,x) counts these selections, giving each selection of size k the weight xᵏ. Evaluating at a negative number means signed cancellation, not negative numbers of selections. Alikhani and Griswold had supplied a connected 33-vertex graph H with minus four as a simple root, meaning the corresponding factor occurs exactly once. Their question was whether infinitely many connected examples exist. Disjoint copies already multiply the polynomial, but leave the network disconnected.

The crucial observation concerns a leaf, a vertex with exactly one neighbor, and that neighbor, its support. Join disjoint graphs only by new edges between supports that retain their own leaves. In any dominating selection, either a leaf or its support must be selected, so the support is already covered within its original graph. Other vertices gain no outside neighbors. Consequently, restricting a dominating selection to each original graph still gives an internally dominating selection. Conversely, uniting such selections dominates the joined graph. This reversible correspondence preserves selection sizes, so the entire polynomial remains the product of the component polynomials. The authors present this as a direct special case of earlier irrelevant-edge theory by Kotek, Preen, Simon, Tittmann and Trinks, not as a newly discovered general principle.

For a small illustration, a single edge has three dominating selections: either endpoint alone, or both endpoints. Its polynomial is 2x+x², which equals eight at minus four. Attach this edge to H by connecting an endpoint to H’s specified leaf-support vertex. The resulting connected 35-vertex graph therefore retains the simple root. Instead, joining r copies of H through their supports along a path gives D(Gᵣ,x)=D(H,x)ʳ. Thus minus four has multiplicity exactly r. The authors obtain 33r vertices, 37r−1 edges and maximum degree seven: the attachment vertices acquire at most two new neighbors, while the original degree-seven vertex is unchanged. Different orders guarantee nonisomorphic examples.

To fill all larger orders, use one H together with a single-edge graph for each increment of two vertices and a three-vertex path for an increment of three. The path’s middle vertex is a support; its polynomial is x+3x²+x³, which equals minus twenty at minus four. Joining these supports along a path again multiplies the polynomials. Every increment of at least two is a sum of twos and threes, giving every order at least 35. These examples retain a simple root and maximum degree seven. Their cycle rank, the number of independent cycles calculated as edges minus vertices plus one for a connected graph, stays four. The authors also establish that all their displayed families containing at least one H have exactly the integer roots zero, minus two and minus four.

The base calculation is finite rather than an enumeration of all selections of 33 vertices. Removing a four-vertex cycle leaves eight smaller pieces, each attached through a root with its own leaf. The authors tabulate each piece’s weighted dominating selections according to whether its root is selected. They then combine these values with selections on the cycle, retaining exactly those combinations covering every cycle vertex. This uses 608 component subsets and 4,096 cycle/root states. They report exact cancellation at minus four, a nonzero derivative confirming the previously known simple root, and a factorization with a rational-root check establishing the integer-root claim. Reported exact polynomial comparisons for 97 joins of at most 12 vertices supplement, but do not replace, the general correspondence proof.

The manuscript distinguishes these written arguments and finite calculations from its stated Lean evidence. The cited declarations cover an integer-weighted decomposition identity, transfer from component values and a vanishing core sum, and existence of connected examples beyond every size bound. That existence statement uses a separate hub-and-leaf construction with 33r+2 vertices. The full polynomial product identity, precise multiplicities, degree bounds and every-order construction have written proofs here; they are not claimed as those Lean declarations’ conclusions. The particular advance is therefore a connected, bounded-degree extension of Alikhani and Griswold’s example, with explicit size control. It answers their infinite-family question, not the general question about other integer roots or the minimum possible order.
